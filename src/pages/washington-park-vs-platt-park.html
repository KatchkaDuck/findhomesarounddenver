Washington Park vs. Platt Park: Which Is Right for You?

They share a border, a high school and the same tree-lined feel, but they live differently and are priced very differently. Here's how the two compare for a family deciding between them.

By Chris Nowakiwsky, Broker Associate, RE/MAX ProfessionalsNovember 23, 2026

The short answer is that Washington Park is the choice if you want the park itself at the end of your street and a bit more house and lot, and you have the budget for it, while Platt Park is the choice if you want to walk to restaurants and the light rail and get into a great central neighborhood for a lot less. Over the last 12 months, the median single-family home in Washington Park sold for about $1.45 million, compared with about $985,000 in Platt Park, according to REcolorado. Both neighborhoods sit a few minutes from each other in South Central Denver, where I live, so it makes sense to tour both before you decide.

The gap in the median price is wider than the gap in price per square foot, which tells you that a lot of the difference comes down to size. Washington Park homes tend to be larger and sit on bigger lots, and the neighborhood has more of the big new builds that pull the median up. Platt Park's townhome and condo median is based on only nine sales, so I wouldn't read too much into it.

The Homes

Both neighborhoods were mostly built between about 1900 and 1930, so you'll see Craftsman bungalows, brick Denver Squares and Tudors in each one, along with modern homes built where an older house was scraped. In Washington Park, lots run from about 25 to 50 feet wide, and the blocks east of Downing Street have more of the larger homes and wider lots. In Platt Park, lots are more often narrow, garages are usually detached and off the alley, and you'll find more duplexes with rooftop decks mixed in between the original bungalows.

In both places, an updated original and a new build can sell far apart on the same block, so the neighborhood median only gets you so far. When I'm helping a family compare, I look at the specific blocks they like and what has sold on them recently, because that's where the real price of a house is set.

Everyday Life

Washington Park

The park is the reason people move here. It covers about 165 acres, with two large lakes, flower gardens that include a replica of Martha Washington's garden at Mount Vernon, tennis courts, a recreation center and a 2.6-mile loop that's full of runners, strollers and bikes from early morning on. Old South Gaylord Street has the neighborhood's coffee shops and restaurants, and the Bonnie Brae shops are a short walk east. The trade-off is that the park draws a crowd, so traffic and parking on the streets around it get busy on summer weekends.

Platt Park

Life in Platt Park revolves around South Pearl Street, a few blocks of independent restaurants, coffee shops and small stores, with a farmers market on Sundays in season and events through the year, including Winterfest in early December. James H. Platt Park in the middle of the neighborhood has a playground, a recreation center and the Decker Branch Library, a Carnegie library from 1913 that's still open. Washington Park is only a few blocks east, so Platt Park families use it all the time without paying Wash Park prices to live next to it.

Schools and Getting Around

Denver Public Schools serving Washington Park include Steele Elementary and Merrill Middle School, and Platt Park is served by McKinley-Thatcher Elementary and Grant Beacon Middle School. Both neighborhoods generally feed South High School. Boundaries vary by address and change over time, so I always have buyers confirm a specific home's assigned schools with DPS before they write an offer.

For commuting, Platt Park has the edge. The Louisiana–Pearl light rail station sits just north of the neighborhood, which makes getting downtown without a car easy, and I-25 is a few blocks away. Washington Park is about 10 minutes from downtown by car, with I-25 along its south edge, and most daily errands are walkable or bikeable from either neighborhood.

What This Market Means for Each

This fall, buyers are taking their time and there are more price reductions and more homes to choose from than we've seen in years. For a family moving up, that changes the math in both neighborhoods. In Platt Park, it can mean a real chance to negotiate on a home that would have drawn multiple offers a few years ago. In Washington Park, it helps to know that across the metro, detached homes priced from $1 million to $1.5 million had about 3.9 months of inventory in October, according to the DMAR Market Trends Report, which is far more balanced than the seller's market of a few years ago, so buyers have more room to talk about price or concessions.

If you own in either neighborhood and are thinking about selling, the same conditions mean pricing and presentation matter more than they have in a while. Well-prepared homes on good blocks are still selling, but buyers are comparing carefully and are quick to pass on a house that feels overpriced. Common Questions

Is Washington Park or Platt Park More Expensive?

Washington Park. Over the 12 months through October 2026, the median single-family home sold for about $1.45 million there, compared with about $985,000 in Platt Park, according to REcolorado. On a price-per-square-foot basis the gap is smaller, about $551 versus $483, because Washington Park homes and lots tend to be larger.

Which Neighborhood Is More Walkable?

Both are very walkable. Platt Park has South Pearl Street and the Louisiana–Pearl light rail station close by, while Washington Park has the park itself and Old South Gaylord Street. Many Platt Park residents walk to Washington Park in a few minutes.

Do Washington Park and Platt Park Share Schools?

They have different elementary and middle schools but generally feed the same high school, South High School. Boundaries vary by address, so confirm a specific home's assigned schools with Denver Public Schools.
