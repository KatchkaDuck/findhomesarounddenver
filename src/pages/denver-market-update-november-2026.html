Denver Real Estate Market Update: November 2026

Prices are holding, but fewer homes are selling and most sales now come with a concession. The deal is getting made in the negotiation, not on the list price.

Here's the short version: prices are steady, sales are slow. The median price for a single-family home in the metro was $645,000 in October, within 1% of a year ago. Condos and townhomes came in at $375,000, down about 3%. Pending sales fell about 7% from September and about 20% from last October, so the slowdown in closings will likely carry into December.

Inventory dipped about 4% from September, which is normal as listings expire heading into the holidays. It's still higher than this time last year, and condos and townhomes have more than seven months of supply.

What I'm Seeing on the Ground

The headline numbers look calm, and that's a little misleading. Homes are still closing at more than 98% of list price, but that figure doesn't capture what happens after the offer. Nearly two out of three October sales included a seller concession, usually money toward the buyer's closing costs or an interest rate buydown. DMAR's Market Trends Committee chair called concessions "a routine part of the negotiation rather than an exception."

That matches what I'm seeing in central Denver. More price reductions, more homes to choose from, and buyers who are in no hurry. With the average 30-year mortgage rate at 7.18% in early November, according to Freddie Mac, every buyer is doing math on the monthly payment, and a concession that lowers it can matter more than a slightly lower price.

Sellers who list now usually have a good reason to. The ones doing well priced the home right on day one and planned for the negotiation before the first showing.

The $1 Million-Plus Market Is Splitting

Through September, homes over $1 million were selling faster than the market overall, and that's still true for well-priced homes in neighborhoods like Washington Park and Bonnie Brae. Higher up, it's a different market. According to the October report, homes above $2 million have about eight months of inventory, firmly in buyer's-market territory, and they're taking longer to go under contract.

For move-up families shopping between $1 million and $2 million, that gap is worth knowing. If your budget stretches toward the top of that range, you'll often find more room to negotiate than you would on a house at $900,000.

What It Means for Sellers

  • Plan for a concession. If most buyers are asking for help with closing costs or a rate buydown, build that into your pricing plan instead of treating it as a surprise.
  • A buydown can beat a price cut. Money toward a buyer's rate can lower their payment more than the same amount off the price. It's worth running both numbers before you reduce.
  • Price for today. With pending sales down 20% from a year ago, an overpriced listing will sit through the holidays. Your competition is the homes on the market now, not last spring's sales.
  • If you can wait, prepare. Use the next two months for repairs, a pre-listing inspection and photos so you're ready when buyers come back in January.

What It Means for Buyers

  • Ask for a concession. Most sellers are already offering one. A credit toward closing costs or a rate buydown is a normal request right now, not an aggressive one.
  • Holiday shopping has its perks. Fewer buyers tour homes between Thanksgiving and New Year's, and the sellers who stay on the market usually need to move.
  • Look at homes that have been sitting. Condos and townhomes, with more than seven months of supply, and homes above $2 million have the most room to negotiate.
