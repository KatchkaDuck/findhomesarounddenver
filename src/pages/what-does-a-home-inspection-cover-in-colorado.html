What Does a Home Inspection Cover in Colorado?

A standard inspection looks at the visible condition of the whole house, from the roof down to the foundation. In Denver, the most important findings often come from the tests you add on top of it.

By Chris Nowakiwsky, Broker Associate, RE/MAX ProfessionalsNovember 16, 2026

A general home inspection in Colorado is a visual, non-invasive look at a home's major systems: the roof, structure and foundation, plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling, water heater, attic, insulation, windows, doors and built-in appliances. It does not usually include a radon test, a camera inspection of the sewer line, mold testing or a check for asbestos, so in an older Denver neighborhood most of my buyers add at least a radon test and a sewer scope, and a mold test when there are signs of past water.

Home inspections provide you with a comprehensive view of the condition of the home. As a buyer, you shouldn't skip them because it gives you peace of mind as you make one of the largest investments of your life. As a seller, it helps you avoid any surprises.

What a Standard Inspection Checks

The inspector walks the house and reports on anything that isn't working as it should, is near the end of its life or could be a safety concern. A typical report covers:

  • Roof and exterior: shingles, flashing, gutters, siding, grading and drainage away from the house.
  • Structure: the foundation, basement walls and framing that can be seen, including cracks and signs of movement or water.
  • Plumbing: supply and drain lines, fixtures, the water heater and visible leaks.
  • Electrical: the panel, wiring that can be seen, outlets and safety devices.
  • Heating and cooling: the furnace or boiler and the air conditioner, when the weather allows them to be tested.
  • Interior: walls, ceilings, floors, windows, doors, the attic and insulation.

What the inspector can't see, they can't report on. Nothing gets opened up, furniture isn't moved, and anything behind walls or under carpet is usually noted as a limitation rather than inspected. It's also worth knowing that Colorado doesn't license home inspectors, so I'd always ask about an inspector's experience, certifications and insurance before you book.

The Add-Ons I Recommend in Denver

Radon Test

Radon is a naturally occurring gas that comes up from the soil, and it's common along the Front Range. The American Lung Association reports that about 46% of radon test results in Colorado are at or above the EPA's action level of 4 pCi/L. The Colorado purchase contract itself notes that the state health department strongly recommends a radon test before buying, and mitigation if levels are high. A mitigation system is a common, fixable item, and it's often a reasonable thing to ask a seller to install or credit.

Sewer Scope

In Denver, the homeowner owns the sewer line from the house out to the city main, including the part under the sidewalk and street. Many homes in neighborhoods like Platt Park, Washington Park and South Park Hill were built roughly a century ago, and their original clay lines can crack, separate or fill with tree roots from the same mature trees that make those streets so beautiful. A sewer scope sends a camera down the line so you can see its condition before you own it, and a broken line can be one of the most expensive repairs in an older home.

Mold Test

Colorado's dry climate doesn't make a home immune to mold. It tends to show up wherever water has gotten in: a basement that has taken on water, a slow leak under a sink or behind a shower, a roof leak in the attic, or a bathroom without good ventilation. A standard inspector will point out visible staining and signs of moisture, but they generally don't test for mold, so a mold test is something you add when you want a clearer answer.

I'd consider one when you notice a musty smell, water stains or past water damage in the disclosure, or when a basement has been finished over an older foundation. If you can already see mold, the EPA notes that testing usually isn't necessary, and the more useful step is finding and fixing the moisture source. There are also no federal limits for mold, so it's worth hiring someone experienced in sampling who can explain what the results mean for that particular house.

Other Tests Worth Considering

  • Lead-based paint: for homes built before 1978, federal law gives buyers the chance to test, and the Colorado contract has its own deadline for it.
  • Structural engineer: if the inspector flags foundation cracks or movement, an engineer can tell you whether it's settling or something more serious. Some Front Range soils swell and shrink with moisture, which can show up in basements.
  • Roofer or HVAC technician: for an older roof, possible hail damage or a furnace near the end of its life, a specialist can give you a firm repair or replacement price.

How the Inspection Window Works in Colorado

Colorado's standard Contract to Buy and Sell Real Estate gives buyers a set period to inspect at their own expense, and the dates are negotiated as part of the offer. Within that period you have three main choices:

  • Terminate by the Inspection Termination Deadline if the home isn't right for you, for any reason related to its condition.
  • Object by the Inspection Objection Deadline, listing the items you'd like the seller to fix or credit.
  • Resolve those items with the seller in writing by the Inspection Resolution Deadline. If you can't agree by then, the contract ends unless you withdraw your objection.

The contract also sets a Seller's Property Disclosure Deadline, by which the seller has to deliver the state disclosure form, and sellers must share any radon test results they know of. I like to set that deadline early so you can read the disclosure before your inspection, and you and your inspector know where to look.

Using the Inspection in Today's Market

A few years ago many buyers waived inspections or promised not to ask for anything. That's not the market we're in now. With more homes for sale and buyers taking their time, sellers are far more willing to negotiate, and DMAR reported that 65% of Denver-area sales in October included a seller concession, averaging $12,557.

That doesn't mean every item on the report belongs in your objection. I'd focus on safety issues, systems that have failed or are close to it, and anything expensive like a sewer line, a roof or a foundation repair, and leave the small, cosmetic items alone. A credit toward closing costs or a rate buydown is often easier for both sides than asking the seller to manage repairs before closing.

If you're inspecting this winter, plan around the weather. Snow can hide a roof, and inspectors often won't run an air conditioner in cold temperatures, so ask how they'll handle anything they can't fully test.

If You're Selling, Inspect First

For sellers, the inspection period is where a lot of deals wobble. A pre-listing inspection, or at least a sewer scope and radon test, lets you fix problems on your own terms or price them in, so a buyer's report doesn't come as a surprise. Common Questions

Is a Home Inspection Required in Colorado?

No. An inspection is the buyer's choice and is paid for by the buyer, but the standard Colorado contract gives you the right to one, and I'd recommend it on every purchase, including new construction.

Does a Home Inspection Include Radon in Colorado?

Usually not. Radon testing is typically an add-on, and many inspectors offer it alongside the general inspection. Given how common elevated radon is in Colorado, I'd always include it.

Do I Need a Sewer Scope in Denver?

For an older home, I'd say yes. Denver homeowners are responsible for the line from the house to the city main, and a camera inspection is the only way to see its condition before you buy.

Can I Back Out After the Inspection in Colorado?

Yes, if you act before the Inspection Termination Deadline in your contract and haven't already sent an inspection objection. Once you object, the process moves to the resolution deadline instead.
